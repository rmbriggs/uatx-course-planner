AMCV 200: The American Founding

(3 Credits; Faculty: Wolf, Reznick, Hoffpauir)

Course Description:
REQUIRED FOR ALL SOPHOMORES. This course examines the intellectual, political,
religious, and cultural origins of the American republic from the colonial period through the
Founding. Students engage directly with primary sources and in particular The Federalist
Papers as they study the principles, institutions, and debates that emerged in Puritan New
England, developed during the colonial era, and culminated in the American Revolution and the
ratification of the U.S. Constitution. Through careful examination of political and religious
writings as well as historical documents, literature, and visual art students consider the complex
relationship between ideals, institutions, and culture that produced one of history's most
distinctive and enduring achievements in constitutional self-government.


AMCV 345: The Legacy of Slavery

(3 Credits; Faculty: Hughes)

Course Description:
The “legacy of slavery” is a phrase we have all heard in history classrooms, on cable news, and
even in presidential speeches. But what does it mean? And why does it matter? For the
purposes of this class, we will define the “legacy of slavery” as the long-term consequences of
American slavery that can still be seen, felt, or measured today. This definition may not be
controversial. The controversy comes when people claim that specific features of modern
American society fall under this definition. For instance, is America’s national wealth the result of
slavery? How about mass incarceration or racial inequality? Students will become acquainted
with two broad schools of thought on the legacy of slavery. The maximalist school sees the
fingerprints of slavery in many aspects of American society, while the minimalist school argues
the opposite: America’s modern problems have little to do with the legacy of slavery.

AMCV 415: Great Events of American History: The Vietnam War

(3 Credits; Faculty: Scheall)

Course Description:
This course examines the decisions of Western policymakers that led to the First Indochina War
between France and the Viet Minh in the immediate wake of World War II, and, later, to the
more famous Vietnam War between North Vietnam and (with varying kinds and degrees of
American assistance) South Vietnam. The course traces the historical progression of the two
wars from France’s failed efforts to reconquer Indochina, the Kennedy administration's various
missteps, the escalation of the war under the Johnson Administration, the disaster of the Tet
Offensive, Nixon and Kissinger's several errors of strategy and diplomacy, through to the final
fall of Saigon. Students will analyze primary sources and consider historical episodes to explore
how limited knowledge, a technocratic approach to military engagement, and political hubris
shaped the war's trajectory.

                                            History

HIST 110: Ancient Greece

(3 Credits; Faculty: Bay, Levy, Allison, Priou)

Course Description:
REQUIRED FOR ALL FRESHMEN. This course immerses students in the history and thought
of the Classical Greek world. By engaging with the most influential ancient Greek historians,
dramatists, and philosophers, students gain an appreciation for one of the ancient foundations
of modern civilization. Readings include Aeschylus’ Persians, portions of the histories of
Herodotus and Thucydides, passages from the Presocratic philosophers, Sophocles’ Oedipus
the King and Antigone, and Plato’s Protagoras and Apology. This survey of Hellenic culture and
literature, which includes attention to historical context and the formative reception of these texts
and authors, prepares students to recognize the contributions made by ancient Greece and
Athens in particular to the Western tradition and human culture writ large.


HIST 111: Ideological Experiments of the 20th Century

(3 Credits; Faculty: Yudelman, Rotner)

Course Description:
REQUIRED FOR ALL SOPHOMORES NOT TAKING ANCIENT ROME THIS WINTER TERM.
This course examines the historical context, intellectual origins, and practical consequences of
the most influential ideological alternatives to liberalism in the twentieth century, with particular
attention to Soviet Russia, Nazi Germany, Fascist Italy, and Communist China. What economic,
social, and cultural conditions made communism, Nazism, and fascism seem appealing? How
did these radical political innovations play out in practice? Students read influential primary
sources such as Marx and Engels’ Communist Manifesto, Gentile and Mussolini’s Doctrine of
Fascism, and Mao’s “Sixteen Points” alongside narrative accounts of historical events such as
the Russian Revolution, the Nazi takeover of Germany, and the Chinese Cultural Revolution.


HIST 115: Ancient Rome

(3 Credits; Faculty: Bove)

Course Description:
REQUIRED FOR ALL SOPHOMORES WHO HAVE NOT ALREADY TAKEN ANCIENT ROME.
This course is a general introduction to the cultural and intellectual history of ancient Rome from
its founding to the high Empire. Why was ancient Rome able to conquer and rule most of the
Mediterranean world? What did Ancient Romans believe? What kind of debates did they have?
What is distinctive about ancient Roman culture, and how did it change over time? Through
independent reading, guided reading, lectures, and in-class discussion of Livy, Cicero, Virgil,
Seneca, Plutarch, and other canonical Roman authors, students in this course acquire a
substantial introductory knowledge of the cultural, intellectual, and political history of ancient
Rome. Students also examine pivotal events such as the expulsion of the Tarquins, the Conflict
of the Orders, the Punic Wars, and the Roman Civil Wars.
HIST 220: Decadence, Modernism, and the Great War

(3 Credits; Faculty: Deadman)

Course Description:
How are twentieth-century political movements such as anarchism, Marxism, fascism, and
Futurism related to each other? What prompted early twentieth-century experiments in art and
architecture such as Dada, Surrealism, Cubism, Art Nouveau, and Art Deco? How did the film
industry begin? This course is a general introduction to the turbulent intellectual, cultural, and
political landscape of England, Europe, and Russia at the end of the nineteenth century and the
beginning of the twentieth century. What led to the end of the Belle Époque? Students
investigate how unprecedented prosperity, hopes for a lasting peace, and fin-de-siècle
decadence gave way to the “Great War” we now know as World War I, as well as the Russian
Revolution and the Spanish Civil War.

HIST 355: Modern Judaism

(3 Credits; Faculty: Kimche)

Course Description:
This course examines the major movements, debates, and crises that have shaped modern
Judaism from the eighteenth century to our present time. Beginning with the Jewish
Enlightenment and Hasidism, it traces the emergence of Jewish responses to emancipation,
secularization, nationalism, philosophy, religious reform, and modern politics. Students explore
the development of modern religious philosophy and analyze how major Jewish thinkers
engaged with concepts such as reason, tradition, ethics, revelation, and collective identity. The
course concludes with the theological and intellectual aftermath of the Holocaust and the
creation of the State of Israel. Through sustained engagement with primary texts, students
explore the various ways modern Jews have sought to reinterpret an ancient tradition under the
pressures of modernity.


HIST 375: The Changing Structure of Civilization

(3 Credits; Faculty: Deadman)

Course Description:
This course examines nineteenth- and twentieth-century theories of civilization that reject the
modernization thesis: the view that history advances through a rational process of development
culminating in secular, liberal, post-ethnonational societies. The thinkers studied here instead
understand civilizations as historically particular forms shaped by ethnos, religion, tradition,
class and elite formation, and cultural “style.” Some are profoundly pessimistic, diagnosing
inevitable cycles of rise and decline; others seek the renewal of the West through the
establishing of “alternative” modernities. By situating these thinkers within a continuous tradition
of Counter-Enlightenment thought, the course asks whether modernity represents progress or
exhaustion and whether Enlightenment universalism marks the culmination of European
civilization or its dissolution. Students learn to historicize twentieth-century radical and
revolutionary movements, reconstruct the civilizational diagnoses from which they emerged, and
explain their appeal to contemporaries.

HIST 385A: Special Topic in History: Education

(3 credits; Faculty: Wright)

We will start with a candidate assessment of education in America today, from K-12 to
undergraduate and graduate higher education, with apprenticeships and AI autodidactism
layered on. Then we ask how we got to this state of affairs and answer it by going back to the
beginning, tracing both literacy and numeracy acquisition from the ancient civilizations to the
modern period, when we hone in on Anglo-American educational institutions, pedagogies, and
traditions until in the final weeks concentrating our gaze upon the U.S.A. in the 19th, 20th, and
21st centuries. With visiting professor Bob Wright.

HIST 385B: Special Topic in History: Journalism

(3 credits: Faculty: Wright)

We will start with a candidate assessment of journalism today. Then we ask how we got to this
state of affairs and answer it by going back to the beginning, to ancient forms of information
dissemination. We'll hit all the major mileposts, like movable type, the rise of financial markets,
the telegraph, and digital telecomm, while focusing on how institutional change shifted economic
incentives and hence the contours of what differentiates news of "record" from "fake news." With
visiting professor Bob Wright.

PHIL 410: Great Philosophers: Introduction to Leo Strauss

(1.5 Credits; Faculty: Rotner)

Course Description:
An intensive introduction to the thought of Leo Strauss by way of a complete reading of his most
influential book. Natural Right and History opens with the Declaration of Independence and a
question the Declaration cannot answer for itself: are its truths self-evident, or are they the
ideals of one nation at one moment in history? Is there a standard of right by which societies,
our own included, may be judged — or is every such standard the creature of the society that
holds it? What happens to political life, and to political science, when the answer is thought to
be “no”? Strauss argues that modern thought has arrived at that no along two roads —
historicism and the distinction between facts and values — and that the consequence is not
liberation but a peculiar helplessness before the very questions that matter most. The course
follows his argument from the crisis backward to its origins: the discovery of nature by the first
philosophers, the classic doctrine of natural right and its account of the best regime, the decisive
break made by Hobbes and completed by Locke, and the crisis of modern natural right as it
appears in Rousseau and Burke.


PHIL 415: Great Theologians: Maximus the Confessor

(3 Credits; Faculty: Constas)

Course Description:
This course is designed to introduce students to major themes in the thought and theology of
Maximos the Confessor, a late-antique ascetic, mystic, and theological writer considered one of
the greatest theological thinkers in the entire Christian tradition. Our primary focus will be on the
Confessor’s two most important works, the Ambigua to John and the Responses to Thalassios.
The former is a series of questions and answers on difficult (or “ambiguous”) passages in the
writings of the Church Fathers; the latter is a similar series of questions and answers on difficult
passages in sacred Scripture. Themes under consideration include Maximos’ transformation of
the Alexandrian spiritual tradition (primarily Origen and Evagrius); theological anthropology;
moral psychology (the life of virtue, the passions); the anagogical exegesis of Scripture;
asceticism; and spirituality.


PHIL 425: Applied Ethics: Design-Thinking Surveillance, Empire, and Consent

(3 Credits; Faculty: Erdman)

Course Description:
Using design-thinking as an analytical and practical framework, this course invites students to
critically examine contemporary technology surveillance as an outcome of over two centuries of
free-enterprise systems and empire-building. It situates current digital practices within longer
histories of resource extraction, debt regimes, and the commodification of data, connecting
events from the Opium Wars to Web 3.0. Grounded in the writings of Ray Dalio, Shoshana
Zuboff, Edward Bernays, Marshall McLuhan, and others, the course reveals how economic,
political, and technological forces intersect to shape modern digital life. Each week integrates
seminar-style historical and theoretical discussion with structured, hands-on design activities
developed by IDEO, Frog, and McKinsey Design. By the end of the course, students will be able
to analyze technology surveillance through historical and systemic lenses, articulate the ethical
and social implications of data-driven business models, and design concept-level interventions
that foreground human agency and digital sovereignty.With visiting professor Charles Erdman.

most accomplished work of science fiction, "though it has behind it a serious 'point.'" Part
Arthurian legend, part interplanetary adventure, and part cautionary tale about the imminent
dangers of technological overreach, That Hideous Strength is an outlandish work of fiction that
can nevertheless seem chillingly close to the reality of the 21st century. At its heart, wrote
George Orwell in his appreciative review of the book, is a company of scientists who have
"destroyed in themselves all human feeling, all notion of good and evil." Students will discuss
and debate the plausibility of Lewis's dark vision, reading That Hideous Strength alongside
excerpts from his other fictional and non-fictional works. No previous knowledge of Lewis is
required.

WRIT 385A: Special Topic in Writing: Film Studio

(3 Credits; Faculty: Webman)

Course Description:
This course introduces students to modern filmmaking and digital media in an era defined by
rapid technological change. Through hands-on exercises, visual analysis, and short creative
projects, students will learn the fundamentals of storytelling, cinematography, editing, and
directing, while critically engaging emerging AI tools that are reshaping creative workflows. The
course emphasizes adaptability, aesthetic clarity, collaborative discipline, and the enduring value
of human creativity. As a two-week intensive course, the pace is accelerated. Students will be
creating and iterating daily. Students who took this course for 1.5 credits last year may take it
